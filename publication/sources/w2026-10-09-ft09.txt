Design and participants excerpt (Sections 3.1–3.2, p. 6):
Four intact third-grade classrooms participated: two formed the experimental group and two the control group. … Participants were 93 third-grade students enrolled in four classrooms at a primary school in Türkiye. … The experimental group included 46 students (24 and 22 in the two classrooms), and the control group included 47 students (23 and 24). … The study was conducted in the spring semester of the 2025–2026 school year.

Intervention excerpt (Sections 3.3–3.4, pp. 6–9):
The experimental group participated in an eight-week integrated STEM sequence supported by a programmable construction and AI kit (LEGO Education Computer Science & AI). … Each weekly activity was delivered in two sessions (about 105–135 min in total) according to a written implementation guide (Appendix A; File S2) and followed the cycle in Figure 1. … The classroom teachers observed and supported but did not teach, and no teacher professional development was included, so the findings reflect learning under expert facilitation (Section 7).

Control condition excerpt (Section 3.5, p. 9):
The two control classrooms continued regular Life Studies lessons during the same eight weeks, taught by their own teachers with the national curriculum and textbook.

Instruments and data excerpt (Section 3.6, pp. 9–10):
Computational thinking was measured with the Turkish adaptation of the Competent Computational Thinking Test. … Because no available standardized measure matched both the age group and the intervention’s conceptual aims, an eight-task performance assessment was developed. … Post-intervention focus-group interviews involved 12 students selected to represent high, moderate, and limited development in AI literacy.

Limitations excerpt (Section 7, p. 19):
Participants came from four intact classrooms in one school, individual random assignment was not possible, and the small number of classroom clusters prevented robust multilevel modeling. … The control condition was business-as-usual rather than an active comparison, so the contribution of the AI–automation contrast cannot be separated from that of additional technology-rich instruction, and instruction was delivered by the second author rather than the classroom teachers, so the results reflect expert facilitation. … The intervention lasted eight weeks and did not include a delayed posttest, so retention and transfer to everyday commercial AI remain unknown. … The machine learning activities focused mainly on pose classification. … The AI Literacy Performance Assessment was developed for this study and closely aligned with the intervention.

Second-round additions (verbatim; Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 7):
The study used an embedded mixed-methods, quasi-experimental pretest–posttest … Convenience sampling was used because the intervention required continuing access to a programmable construction and AI environment, suitable infrastructure, regular attendance, and permission to document classroom processes. … the small number of classroom clusters prevented robust multilevel modeling. Unmeasured classroom or teacher differences may therefore have contributed to the results. The control condition was business-as-usual rather than an active comparison, so the contribution of the AI–automation contrast cannot be separated from that of additional technology-rich instruction … Formal inter-rater and inter-coder agreement coefficients were not computed; … the novelty of the materials may have influenced motivation.

Joint-review additions 2026-10-17 (verbatim, Abstract p.1; 4.2 p.12; 5.1 p.16):
Qualitative evidence showed a shift from viewing AI through robots and automatic actions toward reasoning about learning from examples, data diversity, limitations, human oversight, accessibility, and problem–technology fit. … AI literacy increased markedly in the experimental group and only slightly in the control group (Table 2). After pretest performance was controlled, the adjusted posttest difference was significant … Computational thinking showed the same direction of change, although the effect was smaller. … The magnitude of the AI literacy effect should be read in relation to this close alignment among curriculum, activity, and assessment. … The result does not imply that brief exposure to any AI platform will produce comparable gains.
