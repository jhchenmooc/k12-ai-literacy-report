Passage (Abstract, sentences 1–3):
This study reports survey findings on Vietnamese secondary teachers’ use, perceptions and professional learning needs regarding Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) in argumentative writing instruction (Years 6–9). Based on responses from 515 teachers, the analysis examines GenAI applications across the writing cycle, perceived impacts on student learning, barriers to adoption and areas for support. Results show widespread awareness of GenAI and clear preferences for tools such as ChatGPT, which teachers mainly use for planning and modelling tasks rather than for feedback or assessment.
Passage (Abstract, sentences 4–5):
Most teachers view GenAI as helpful for improving students’ ability to plan and develop arguments, but challenges include time constraints, limited training and concerns about tool quality and academic integrity. Professional learning needs are significant, especially among mid- and senior-career teachers and vary by school context.
Passage (2. Literature review, last paragraph):
To address the gap mentioned, the present study employs a survey design to explore Vietnamese teachers’ perspectives on GenAI in argumentative writing instruction. Given the limited empirical research on GenAI use in secondary writing classrooms, the study is exploratory in nature, with the aim of documenting teachers’ reported practices and perceptions rather than testing predefined hypotheses.
Passage (3. Methodology, para. 1):
An online survey was administered to secondary school teachers across Vietnam to gather their perceptions of GenAI use in argumentative writing instruction. The survey included 44 questions organised into five thematic blocks (Table 1).
Passage (3. Methodology, para. 1 (instrument)):
The survey instrument was reviewed by five experts in Vietnamese language education, educational measurement and educational technology to establish content validity, and the wording and structure of several items were refined based on their feedback. The instrument was then piloted with 39 secondary school teachers who were not included in the main sample.
Passage (3. Methodology, para. 2):
Valid responses were received from 515 teachers, who were recruited using a convenience sampling strategy through professional networks, school clusters and regional training lists. Because the survey link was disseminated through open, cascading channels rather than a fixed distribution list, the total number of teachers who received the invitation could not be determined, and a conventional response rate could not be calculated.
Passage (3. Methodology, para. 2 (analysis)):
Survey data were coded and analysed using SPSS Statistics 27.
Passage (3. Methodology, para. 2 (analysis)):
Descriptive statistics were used to map overall patterns of GenAI use and perceptions and, where appropriate, to explore relationships between teacher demographics, GenAI familiarity and reported practices.
Passage (5. Discussion):
Most Vietnamese secondary school teachers reported learning about GenAI through informal channels such as social media and peer recommendations, which in UTAUT terms reflects social influence operating largely outside formal institutional channels.

Passage (5. Discussion, end):
These implications should be interpreted with caution, as they are based on self-reported data and do not provide direct evidence of impact on student outcomes.
Passage (6. Conclusion, limitations paragraph):
Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the study is based on teachers’ self-reported practices and perceptions, which may not reflect what happens in actual classroom settings. Relatedly, although a large majority of teachers reported frequently verifying GenAI-generated content, the survey instrument measured only the frequency of verification and did not capture how teachers verified outputs – for example, whether they cross-checked against authoritative sources, consulted colleagues, or simply re-read the content for plausibility – which is an important distinction for judging the quality, rather than merely the frequency, of GenAI use. In addition, participants were recruited through voluntary, convenience-based sampling with no defined sampling frame, meaning that a response rate could not be calculated and the sample may be somewhat skewed towards teachers who already have an interest in GenAI. As a cross-sectional study conducted near the end of the school year, the findings offer a snapshot of teachers’ experiences at a particular point in time and cannot capture how these practices and perceptions may change.
