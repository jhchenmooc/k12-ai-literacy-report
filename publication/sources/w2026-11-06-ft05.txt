This two-phase study examined calibrated AI readiness, defined as self-reported readiness to verify AI outputs, recognize ethical boundaries, and retain responsibility for AI-assisted professional decisions.
Passage (3.2.1 Participants and recruitment):
Participants were practicing school psychologists and related school-based professionals, including supervisors, school counselors, teachers and special education professionals working in K-12 settings.

Phase 2 used a single-group pretest–posttest pilot training design to examine feasibility, descriptive change, and the relationship between changes in self-reported calibrated AI readiness and scenario-based responsible AI reasoning.

Passage (5 Discussion):
In Phase 1, AI-related epistemic vigilance capacity and governance clarity were positively associated with self-reported calibrated AI readiness, whereas autonomous regulatory motivation was not a significant direct predictor.
Passage (5 Discussion):
Phase 2 showed descriptive increases in self-reported readiness following a one-month pilot training program, while scenario-based assessments provided separate evidence of applied responsible AI reasoning. However, self-reported changes and scenario-based reasoning were not significantly associated.
Passage (Abstract):
Scenario-based responses showed evidence of verification, privacy protection, contextual evaluation, and human accountability, although performance varied across specific indicators.
Passage (Abstract, last sentence):
Because Phase 1 was cross-sectional and Phase 2 lacked a comparison group, the results do not establish causal effects.
Passage (5.5 Limitations):
First, Phase 1 employed a cross-sectional correlational design, preventing causal conclusions about the relationships among epistemic vigilance capacity, autonomous regulatory motivation, governance clarity, and calibrated AI readiness.
Passage (5.5):
Consequently, selection and nonresponse biases may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly if professionals with greater interest in AI were more likely to participate.
Passage (5.5):
Second, the measures were developed specifically for this study and received preliminary expert feedback but did not undergo formal content-validity assessment or cognitive interviewing.
Passage (5.5):
Accordingly, self-reported calibrated AI readiness should not be interpreted as demonstrated professional competence.
Passage (5.5):
Third, Phase 2 involved only 25 participants and used a single-group pretest–posttest design without a comparison group. The descriptive increases in readiness therefore cannot be attributed causally to the training. High baseline scores, ceiling effects, repeated measurement, and possible response-shift effects may have influenced the observed patterns.
Passage (5.5):
the 13-indicator reasoning index was exploratory and had not undergone independent validation.
