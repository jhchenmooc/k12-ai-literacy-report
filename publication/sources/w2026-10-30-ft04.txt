Abstract excerpt (p. 1, Abstract):
In the present study, 7 middle school mathematics teachers used ChatGPT to create personalized versions of problems in their curriculum to correspond to their students’ interests. The prompting moves teachers made and their students’ open-ended feedback on personalized problems were examined through thematic analysis. Teachers’ efficiency when creating problems and the ratings of their 7th grade students who received personalized assignments were examined. Results from this sample suggest that having a teacher-in-the-loop may result in generative AI-enhanced personalization being enacted at a broad grain size, whereas students often expressed preference for a smaller grain-size where they receive specific popular culture references that interest them. Teachers used prompting to adjust popular culture references and address issues with the depth, ethics, or realism of the problems generated, in some cases showing epistemic agency.

Curriculum excerpt (pp. 2–3, Introduction):
Seven middle school teachers created mathematics problems personalized to their students’ interests using GPT-4o. The teachers began with current problems in their Illustrative Mathematics (IM) curriculum and then modified problems to better match students’ interest areas. IM is a free, open-source mathematics curriculum used in the United States with materials for grades kindergarten through 12th grade. These problems were subsequently assigned to their students (n = 521).

Sample excerpt (pp. 7–8, Method > Sample):
The sample included 7 teachers (mean teaching experience = 11.9 years) posing 143 personalized problems, which were then given to 7th grade students in ASSISTments (Heffernan & Heffernan, 2014), a platform for math homework that includes the IM curriculum. Teachers taught 7th grade mathematics in 6 different US schools; 2 teachers identified as male and 5 as female. … The schools were geographically diverse (East Coast, West Coast, South, Midwest) … Teachers were recruited with email advertisements via ASSISTments and were paid for their study participation. … Parents could either (1) opt-out of research participation for their child, (2) not opt-out, and allow de-identified ASSISTments log data (with 1–5 problem ratings) to be collected from their child, or (3) opt-in to demographic and open-ended feedback data being collected from their child in addition to ASSISTments log data. The 7 teachers taught 521 students whose parents did not opt-out and 13 students whose parents did opt-out. 197 students had the additional layer of opt-in that allowed us to collect demographic information in addition to ASSISTments log data. … The teachers varied in their limited prior reported usage of GenAI—from not using it at all, to using it for communication with families, to occasionally using it to generate problems and worksheets.

Design excerpt (p. 8, Method > Sample):
Because this was an exploratory, design-focused study, the adequacy of the sample size was considered in relation to the richness and repetition observed in the data. The seven teachers contributed 143 problem-design episodes and 558 prompts, providing a substantial corpus for characterizing prompting practices, illustrating recurring design tensions, and generating hypotheses about teacher-in-the-loop personalization. … However, the study was not intended to estimate the prevalence of these practices among mathematics teachers or students generally.

Procedure excerpt (pp. 8–9, Method > Procedure):
Teachers participated in a 2-h training where they learned about research on personalization, explored how to elicit their students’ out-of-school interests, discussed ethical issues of bias, readability, and cultural relevance when personalizing problems, and practiced using ChatGPT to personalize problems. … Teachers then selected up to 8 problems from IM Unit 4 (Proportional Relationships and Percentages). The teachers made either 1 or 2 personalized versions of each problem, using consistent interest areas (e.g., sports or food), using ChatGPT (GPT-4o) to write personalized versions of each problem while being recorded by Zoom. … Students completed the assignments for classwork or homework, and then the researchers and the teacher engaged in a reflection session together to go over student feedback and ratings. … This process of creating and assigning personalized problem sets was then repeated for IM Unit 6 (Expressions, Equations, and Inequalities), with another reflection session taking place after Unit 6.

Measures excerpt (pp. 9–10, Method > Measures):
Teachers’ interaction logs with ChatGPT were analyzed for how many prompts they used and the time they spent interacting with ChatGPT. … Teachers were instructed to explain their actions in ChatGPT to 2 researchers who were present while they worked on personalizing. … For 53 of the 143 personalized problems, all students received two items: (1) “On a scale of 1–5, how interesting did you find this last problem?” and (2) “On a scale of 1–5, how closely did this problem match things you are interested in?” … The subset of students with parental consent received additional open-ended items after approximately 20% of the personalized problems (33 of 143 problems).

Data analysis excerpt (pp. 10–12, Method > Data analysis):
The analysis method was reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019a) with elements of codebook-based thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021), a flexible qualitative approach well suited to diverse data sources and grounded in the recognition that researchers play an active, interpretive role in the analytic process. … For this reason, guidelines describe how the logic behind seeking to obtain measures of inter-rater reliability does not make sense in the context of this method (Braun & Clarke, 2019b, 2021). … The quantitative analyses presented here are correlational in nature, not allowing for the inference of causality.

Results RQ1 excerpt (pp. 12–14, Results > Research question 1):
Moves for the initial prompt included giving ChatGPT the problem and asking it to either change the problem to be about a general popular culture area (“Can you make this problem about food?”) or to add/swap a specific popular culture reference (“Can you rewrite this relating to Lululemon clothes?”). … After the initial prompt, teachers made a total of 415 additional prompts. … Table 2 also shows that teachers used prompts to correct realism issues, which relates to depth. … The teachers would also sometimes use ChatGPT for brainstorming or research, including to check for realism. … The next group of categories involved teachers asking ChatGPT to tailor problems to their students’ age group, geographic location, or typical names. … In some instances, the LLM was prompted to fix the mathematical structures of the problem and in other instances, the general readability of the problem. … There were also instances of ChatGPT generating content that teachers felt was inappropriate or biased, showing important instances where teachers may have been engaging in ethical monitoring. These included problematic celebrities being mentioned, a problem being modified by ChatGPT from being about one boy and one girl to being about two boys, and a scenario tracking a person’s weight.

Results RQ2 excerpt (pp. 14–16, Results > Research question 2):
There were 454 responses to the first student open-ended feedback, 449 to the second question, and 446 to the third question. For the first question, students gave 163 open-ended text responses implying the problem was interesting and 243 responses implying it was not, with the rest being uninformative or answers to a different question. … When they were giving feedback on the problems, there was little evidence that students explicitly attended to the dimension of ownership, especially compared to the degree to which they attended to the dimension of grain size. … Overall, students did not seem as attuned to the depth of the problems when giving feedback, focusing more on surface-level characteristics like popular culture references.

Results RQ3 excerpt (p. 16, Results > Research question 3):
To write the 143 problems with ChatGPT, teachers used 558 prompts, for an average of 3.9 prompts per problem (SD = 2.6). The average problem took 246 s or approximately 4 min (SD = 165.7 s) to write.

Discussion excerpt (pp. 18–19, Discussion):
While teachers can play a key role in the AI-mediated personalization process by engaging in ethical and epistemic monitoring, it is important to acknowledge that placing a teacher in-the-loop does not automatically ensure that these behaviors will take place. … From the qualitative analysis of the student feedback, results suggested that students in the sample often asked for specific, small-grain-size connections to their popular culture interests, and disliked specific references when they do not match with their personal preferences. … Teacher-in-the-loop personalization may be structurally constrained to particular grain sizes due to time costs, independent of pedagogical preferences of teachers and students, but more research is needed to determine this. … When looking at descriptive data on prompting efficiency, exploratory trends suggested there may be a pattern where using a modest number of prompts is associated with the most favorable student feedback. … One important alternative explanation for the findings is that teachers needed more prompts to personalize problems that were more difficult or complex—and that students tended to have greater dislike for more difficult problems.

Limitations excerpt (p. 20, Significance and conclusion):
A limitation of this study is that it only examined proximal outcomes of student interest ratings and open-ended feedback, rather than students’ performance and learning. … This study was designed to be exploratory, so it did not have a control group that would allow for the comparative analysis of performance outcomes; this is an important direction for future work. Limitations also included that teachers were being observed while prompting, which may have shaped prompting behaviors in ways that differ from naturalistic classroom planning contexts. Finally, only seven teachers participated, all of whom taught seventh-grade mathematics and used IM through ASSISTments. … In addition, the teachers engaged in specific training and reflection sessions which would not be the case for a general population of teachers engaging in personalized task creation. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating within this bounded implementation, rather than as estimates of how frequently these practices occur among teachers or as evidence that the observed patterns will transfer to other grades, curricula, platforms, AI models, or national contexts.

Conclusion excerpt (pp. 20–21, Significance and conclusion):
Merely having teachers in-the-loop does not guarantee that teachers will activate their epistemic agency and engage in epistemic monitoring, but it does allow for these behaviors to arise. Within this implementation, the findings suggest a potential scalability tradeoff between teacher control and students’ preference for fine-grained interest matching that has not been documented in prior personalization research.
