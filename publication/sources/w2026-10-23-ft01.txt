Abstract excerpt (p. 1, Abstract):
This study was conducted in a Swedish school that had started the pilot project “AI on the schedule” for compulsory school students, for whom the national curricula do not include AI training. A survey covering the aspects of source evaluation, data privacy, algorithmic manipulation, transparency, responsibility, and governance was distributed to these year 7 students (n = 47). The data was analysed through cross-tabulations of question pairs. This revealed how epistemic agency varied across aspects within individual students. Results show that students demonstrated strong epistemic agency in certain areas but weak agency in others, for example, in data privacy and algorithmic manipulation, even when these had been part of earlier instruction. The contribution of the study is that epistemic agency in AI-mediated practices is not a unified construct: the data suggest distinct output-directed and system-directed dimensions, each with its own area of application.

Context and participants excerpt (p. 3, Method > Context and participants):
The study was conducted in a Swedish compulsory school, which was selected because it had launched a pilot project titled “AI on the schedule.” For the 2025–2026 school year, students in year 7 were offered weekly 90-minute AI training sessions. … One teacher and 47 students participated in the study. To answer the research questions, a survey was distributed at the end of the semester.

Design excerpt (p. 4, Method > Design):
This exploratory mixed-methods case study (Yin, 2018) approaches students’ epistemic agency in AI-mediated practices through three complementary layers of data. Closed survey items capture espoused stances: the positions students select when presented with ordered alternatives. Open-ended motivations attached to each item capture articulated reasoning (i.e., the justifications and heuristics) through which students account for their stances. Classroom observation was used to capture observed responses during instruction and then related interactions.

AI training sessions excerpt (p. 4, Method > AI training sessions before the distribution of the survey):
During the autumn semester the sessions had included the following aspects, referred to as phase one, two and three. … These phases are relevant to the analysis because foregrounding lessons included source evaluation, responsibility, data privacy, algorithmic manipulation, and governance.

Data collection excerpt (p. 4, Method > Data collection and analysis):
The survey (n = 47) was distributed and gathered in November (phase 3). To explore students’ critical reflections on AI use, nine questions were raised. The items were constructed to cover the aspects addressed in the AI training sessions (source evaluation, responsibility, data privacy, algorithmic manipulation, transparency, and governance) and were informed by the literature on epistemic agency. … The survey was not piloted with a separate sample; instead, comprehensibility was addressed by phrasing items and response alternatives in language observed in the classroom. Items were grouped a priori by the epistemic demand they place on the student. Output-directed items (Q1, Q2, Q3) require the student to evaluate AI-generated output against external criteria (Barzilai & Zohar, 2012). System-directed items concern the student’s awareness of system processes: data practices and transparency (Q4, Q6) (Coeckelbergh, 2026), and recognition of algorithmic influence in everyday technology use (Q7) (Coeckelbergh, 2026). Use-directed items (Q5, Q8, Q9) concern responsibility and ownership in AI-mediated work, as epistemic agency presupposes such responsibility (Jose et al., 2025; Scardamalia, 2002).

Response alternatives and analysis excerpt (p. 5, Method > Data collection and analysis):
The response alternatives (A-D) represent increasing levels of epistemic agency: A reflects a passive, uncritical stance (e.g. trusting AI without verification, or indifference to data collection); … In addition, each closed item was followed by an open-ended field (“Briefly explain why you chose your answer”), yielding nine written motivations per respondent with a near-complete completion rate. … the data were therefore treated as ordinal and are reported as distributions, medians, and modes. To examine whether epistemic engagement dissociates across domains within students, a dissociation index was computed for each student as the difference between the median of the output-directed items (Q1, Q2, Q3) and the median of the system-directed items (Q4, Q6, Q7), and evaluated with an exact two-sided sign test. … The written motivations were analysed through qualitative content analysis: responses were read item by item, inductively coded into categories of reasoning (for example, verification heuristics and attributions of responsibility), and compared across item domains. All coding was conducted by the author; … Selected cross-tabulations of item pairs are retained as illustrative displays of how individual students combine stances across domains.

Results excerpt (pp. 6–7, Results > Epistemic agency across aspects of AI use (RQ1)):
As a group, students displayed the strongest epistemic engagement on governance (Q9: Mdn = 4, with 57% selecting the highest alternative), and on source evaluation and responsibility (Q2, Q3, Q5: all Mdn = 3), and the weakest on data privacy (Q4: Mdn = 1, with 66% selecting the lowest alternative) and algorithmic manipulation (Q7: Mdn = 2, with the largest single group, 47%, selecting the lowest alternative). Observed responses excerpt (p. 11, Results > Observed responses and reported stances):
One month later, the survey was distributed. Sixty-six per cent of the student group selected the lowest alternative on data reading (Q4 = A, “Good, then the answers get better”), and 47% selected the lowest alternative on algorithmic influence (Q7 = A, “I do not care, it is just fun”). The observed reactions and the reported stances are group-level data and cannot be linked at the individual level. At group level, however, the juxtaposition shows that the low reported engagement with data practices followed instruction in which those practices had been made directly visible to, and momentarily problematised by, the students.

Discussion excerpt (p. 12, Discussion > Epistemic agency in AI-mediated practices):
Source criticism and system criticism thus operate as separate registers: the capacity to evaluate AI output against external sources does not extend to questioning the data practices through which that output was generated.

Limitations excerpt (pp. 13–14, Limitations and future research):
The study has several limitations: first, the study was conducted in a single Swedish school and therefore does not claim generalisability. Moreover, the survey captured self-reported reflections at a single point in time and does not permit causal claims about the relationship between instruction and epistemic agency. A student who selected “I check where they got the information” may not actually do so; the data reflect how students reason about their practices, not how they enact them. … The response alternatives were ordered from lower to higher engagement, which may have introduced a social desirability effect, particularly on questions where a “correct” answer is socially visible, such as responsibility and governance. This means that the high scores on these questions may overestimate actual epistemic agency. … Cross-tabulations describe patterns of association, not causal relationships, and the sample size limits the statistical power of individual cells. Alternative explanations for the observed patterns cannot be ruled out: aspects in which students scored high, such as source evaluation, may be inherently easier to grasp or have been addressed in other school subjects, while the low scores on data privacy and algorithmic manipulation may reflect developmental factors rather than instructional gaps alone.
