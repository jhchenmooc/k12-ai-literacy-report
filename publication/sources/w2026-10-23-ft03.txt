Abstract excerpt (PDF p. 1, Introduction last sentence; Methods):
The aim of this study was to develop and validate the Artificial Intelligence Self-Efficacy Scale for Teachers (AISES-T). … Data were collected from teachers in different regions of Turkiye within a scale development design. Following expert review, a pool of 50 items was reduced to a 27-item trial form. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring was conducted in a first sample of 313 teachers, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted in a second, independent sample of 559 teachers.

Abstract excerpt (PDF p. 1, Discussion):
The results show that teachers’ AI self-efficacy comprises related but distinguishable beliefs about learning with AI, integrating it into instruction and understanding how it works. The AISES-T offers a valid and reliable measure for research on AI-supported teaching and for planning teacher professional development.

Theory excerpt (PDF p. 4, 1 Introduction, last paragraphs):
Building on Social Cognitive Theory, this study conceptualizes teachers’ AI self-efficacy as a set of domain- and task-specific beliefs concerning their capability to perform AI-related actions in educational contexts.

Theory excerpt (PDF p. 4, 1 Introduction):
In this sense, the AISES-T measures capability beliefs rather than actual performance, factual knowledge, attitudes, behavioral intentions, or expected outcomes.

Research design excerpt (PDF p. 5, 2.1 Research design):
This research is a scale development study conducted to measure teachers’ perceived AI self-efficacy, and a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design was used.

Participants excerpt (PDF p. 5, 2.2 Participants, first sentence):
Participants were teachers employed in schools in different regions of Türkiye.

Participants excerpt (PDF p. 5, 2.2 Participants, second paragraph):
The EFA sample comprised 313 teachers, corresponding to 11.6 respondents per item for the 27-item trial form. The CFA was carried out with a different group of 559 teachers (25.4 respondents per item for the 22-item form). The two samples were collected one after the other through an online questionnaire, and no teacher took part in both. Participation was voluntary, and participants were reached through convenience sampling.

Item development excerpt (PDF p. 5, 2.3 Item development and content validity):
We also examined international frameworks, particularly the UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Teachers (Miao and Cukurova, 2024) and DigCompEdu (Redecker, 2017), so that the items would reflect what teachers are expected to be able to do in AI-supported learning environments. Drawing on this groundwork, 50 items were written and assigned in advance to the three hypothesized dimensions.

Expert panel excerpt (PDF p. 5, 2.3):
The panel comprised 10 experts, namely 2 specialists in educational technology, 2 specialists in educational measurement and evaluation, 2 specialists in curriculum and instruction, 2 specialists in Turkish language and expression, and 2 practising teachers with experience of using AI applications in the classroom.

Discussion excerpt (PDF pp. 16–17, 4.1 A three-dimensional structure of teachers’ AI self-efficacy):
The exploratory and confirmatory analyses consistently indicated that teachers’ AI self-efficacy is better represented by three correlated dimensions than by a single undifferentiated belief (Δχ²(3) = 2,202.35, p < 0.001). … The AISES-T can therefore be interpreted at two levels: the total score provides an index of general AI self-efficacy, whereas the subscale scores provide a more diagnostic profile of the actions that teachers feel more or less capable of performing.

Ethics dimension excerpt (PDF p. 17, 4.4 The place of ethics in teachers’ AI self-efficacy):
A notable feature of the final instrument is that it does not include an ethics dimension, although the ethical use of AI is central to recent competency frameworks (Chiu et al., 2025; Miao and Cukurova, 2024). The five ethics-related items in the initial pool (P26–P30) were removed during expert review because of low I-CVI values (0.10–0.30). Their wording helps to explain this outcome: most expressed attitudes, beliefs or dispositions (e.g., “I believe that human oversight is necessary…”) rather than capabilities, and therefore did not fit the self-efficacy format required by Social Cognitive Theory. Consequently, the AISES-T should not be interpreted as a measure of teachers’ ethical AI self-efficacy; the evaluative component it captures is limited to judging the reliability of AI-generated information (I21).

Practical implications excerpt (PDF p. 18, 4.5 Practical implications, last sentence):
Because the instrument measures beliefs rather than demonstrated competence, it is suited to diagnostic and research purposes and should not be used as a basis for high-stakes decisions about individual teachers.

Limitations excerpt (PDF p. 18, 4.6 Limitations):
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the data were obtained through self-report and may be affected by social desirability and common-method variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003); moreover, self-efficacy beliefs may over- or underestimate actual performance. Second, both samples were recruited through convenience sampling from teachers in Türkiye, which limits the generalisability of the findings to other educational systems and to teachers with different levels of access to AI technologies. Third, the 12 within-factor residual covariances in the final model were identified post hoc from modification indices in the CFA sample; although they did not alter the factor structure, they may reflect sample-specific variance or item redundancy and should be tested in new samples. Fourth, measurement invariance across groups such as gender, school level and teaching experience, as well as test–retest reliability and criterion-related validity, were not examined. Finally, the ethical dimension of AI use is not represented in the final instrument, and given the rapid evolution of AI, particularly generative AI, the relevance of specific item wordings may need periodic review.

Conclusion excerpt (PDF p. 18, 5 Conclusion):
This study developed and validated the 22-item Artificial Intelligence Self-Efficacy Scale for Teachers (AISES-T). Evidence from two independent samples supported a three-factor structure (AI Use and Learning Self-Efficacy, Pedagogical AI Integration Self-Efficacy, and AI Awareness and Understanding Self-Efficacy), together with high internal consistency and satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity.
