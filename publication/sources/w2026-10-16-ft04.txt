Abstract (PDF p. 2 (printed p. 1)): As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes integrated into PK–12 education, teachers need professional judgment to evaluate its fairness. This conceptual paper develops Critical Fairness Literacy (CFL) through a structured synthesis of literature on teacher AI competency, critical AI literacy, algorithmic fairness, educational AI ethics, and educational equity. CFL is proposed as a subdimension of teacher AI competency focused on fairness, comprising bias source identification, contextual equity analysis, and interrogation of fairness definitions. Three practical protocols, bias tracing, equity-centered contextual review, and fairness framing reflection, connect these dimensions to classroom judgment and professional learning. The paper proposes how guided inquiry, peer feedback, and repeated application might support CFL development. The framework and protocols remain theoretically informed proposals requiring empirical investigation.

Design excerpt (Framework development procedure, PDF p. 6 (printed p. 5)): This paper develops Critical Fairness Literacy (CFL) through a structured conceptual synthesis of scholarship on teacher AI competency, critical AI literacy, algorithmic fairness, educational AI ethics, and educational equity. The synthesis aims to develop a theoretically informed framework rather than provide an exhaustive account of the literature or establish an empirically validated competency model.
Sources were identified during spring 2026 through Google Scholar, Web of Science, Scopus, and organizational websites, including UNESCO. Searches used terms related to teacher AI competency, AI literacy, AI readiness, AI adoption, critical AI literacy, algorithmic fairness, educational AI ethics, and educational equity, individually and in combination. Additional sources were located through reference lists and citation searching.
Sources were selected for their contributions to understanding fairness-related teacher responsibilities, potential sources of bias, consequences for learners, or competing fairness standards. Sources that did not contribute to these concerns were excluded from the synthesis. Relevant literature outside PK–12 education provided conceptual support rather than direct evidence about PK–12 teacher competency. References used solely for background were distinguished from sources used in the synthesis.
The development process involved four stages. First, we examined how the selected teacher AI competency frameworks and policy documents address fairness, bias, inclusion, and equity, considering both explicit competencies and responsibilities within broader ethical domains. Conclusions were restricted to the documents examined. Second, we drew on critical AI literacy, algorithmic fairness and bias, educational AI ethics, and educational equity to elaborate fairness-related professional judgment. Third, we compared concepts using questions about potential sources of unfairness, consequences for different learners, and competing fairness standards. Concepts were assessed for relevance to PK–12 teacher judgment, explanatory value, and conceptual distinctiveness. Recurrence across sources informed this assessment but was not used as a frequency threshold. Fourth, we grouped concepts by their evaluative functions, combining overlapping concepts and distinguishing those concerned with bias sources, contextual consequences, and fairness standards. This process yielded three interrelated dimensions: bias source identification, contextual equity analysis, and interrogation of fairness definitions.
Three dimensions translate broad fairness commitments into proposed forms of teacher professional judgment. They represent one theoretically informed synthesis of the selected literature and remain open to conceptual refinement and empirical investigation.

Research questions (Introduction, PDF p. 3 (printed p. 2)): It is guided by three questions: (1) How do the examined teacher AI competency frameworks conceptualize fairness? (2) What dimensions of professional judgment define Critical Fairness Literacy (CFL)? (3) How do professional learning and practical protocols support teachers’ development of CFL?

Findings excerpt (CFL section, PDF pp. 6–7 (printed pp. 5–6)): This paper therefore argues that Critical Fairness Literacy (CFL) should be conceptualized as a distinct subdimension of teacher AI competency. CFL refers to teachers’ capacity to critically evaluate fairness and bias in AI-supported educational practices and make context-sensitive judgments about their use. … In this study, CFL is conceptualized as comprising three interrelated dimensions: bias source identification, contextual equity analysis, and interrogation of fairness definitions. … To avoid conflating competency structure with competency development, the framework distinguishes three analytical levels. First, the dimensions define what CFL entails. Second, a developmental pathway outlines how these capacities may deepen over time. Third, practical protocols translate CFL into questions and actions that teachers can use in specific educational contexts.

Dimension definitions (PDF pp. 7–8 (printed pp. 6–7)): The first dimension of CFL is bias source identification, the capacity to recognize how bias may enter AI-supported educational practices. … The second dimension of CFL is contextual equity analysis, which refers to the capacity to evaluate fairness in relation to the specific educational context in which AI is used. … The third dimension of CFL is interrogation of fairness definitions, which refers to teachers’ capacity to critically examine the standards by which fairness is defined and justified in AI-supported educational practice.

Development excerpt (Developing Critical Fairness Literacy over time, PDF pp. 8–9 (printed pp. 7–8)): CFL is better understood as a developing form of professional judgment that may deepen through guided inquiry and repeated application. … These indicators and the proposed learning processes remain in conceptual propositions rather than validated measures or demonstrated developmental effects.

Protocols excerpt (Practical protocols for enacting Critical Fairness Literacy, PDF pp. 9–12 (printed pp. 8–11)): To operationalize the three dimensions of CFL, this study proposes three practical protocols: bias tracing, equity-centered contextual review, and fairness framing reflection. These protocols translate CFL into structured reflection processes that support teachers in evaluating AI-supported practices from a fairness perspective. … The bias tracing protocol operationalizes the dimension of bias source identification by guiding teachers to examine possible explanations for problematic outputs and how such outputs may become educationally consequential. … The equity-centered contextual review protocol operationalizes the dimension of contextual equity analysis by guiding teachers to evaluate AI use in relation to the learning conditions it is deployed. … The fairness framing reflection protocol operationalizes the dimension of interrogation of fairness definitions by guiding teachers to examine how fairness is being defined in an AI-supported practice and whether that definition is educationally appropriate. … These uses are proposed learning designs rather than demonstrated effects; empirical investigation is needed to determine their usability and whether they strengthen CFL over time.

Illustrative application excerpt (PDF pp. 12–13 (printed pp. 11–12)): To illustrate how CFL may guide teachers’ professional judgment, this section presents a hypothetical classroom scenario involving an AI-assisted feedback tool. The purpose of this example is not to validate CFL empirically, but to demonstrate how the proposed dimensions can inform practical decision making. A middle school English teacher uses a generative AI system to provide feedback on student writing.

Limitations excerpt (Conclusion and implications, PDF p. 14 (printed p. 13)): Several limitations qualify the claims of this paper. First, CFL is developed through conceptual synthesis rather than empirical investigation. Although the framework draws on multiple bodies of scholarship, its proposed dimensions have not yet been validated as distinct competencies among PK–12 teachers. CFL should therefore be understood as a theoretically informed proposition rather than an established competency model. Second, the proposed protocols have not yet been implemented or evaluated in teacher education or professional development settings. Their usability, relevance across contexts, and capacity to support teachers’ professional reasoning require further investigation. The illustrative application demonstrates possible enactment of CFL but does not provide evidence of implementation effectiveness. Third, the framework may require adaptation across educational contexts, including different subject areas, grade levels, AI applications, institutional settings, and student populations.
