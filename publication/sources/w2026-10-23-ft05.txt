Abstract excerpt (p. 1, abstract sentences 3 to 6 and 9):
This cross-sectional study used survey data from 141,988 students in Grades 6, 9, and 11 in Changchun, Northeast China. … The study addressed three research questions: whether AI literacy differed by grade and student background; which family, academic, lifestyle, and media-use factors were associated with it; and whether profiles emerged across AI understanding, use, evaluation, and ethical awareness. … Group comparisons, correlation and hierarchical regression analyses, and latent profile analysis were conducted. … AI literacy was higher among students in the upper grades, urban students, and students from higher-socioeconomic-status families, while gender differences were small. … Four latent profiles were identified: overall low-level, overall high-level, high-exposure–low-reflection, and low-exposure–high-reflection.

Participants excerpt (p. 4, 2.1. Participants):
The data for this study were drawn from the Changchun municipal educational monitoring program, which used cluster sampling to survey students in Changchun, Northeast China. … Students completed paper-and-pencil assessments at school. … The initial sample included 202,893 students, with 75,833 students in Grade 6, 74,563 in Grade 9, and 52,497 in Grade 11. Data screening was conducted before the main analyses. First, the assessment included seven social desirability items, such as “I never cry,” “I have never used bad language,” and “I never lie.” Students with an average score above 0.3 were retained, and schools with an average score above 0.5 were retained. Second, students with a response rate above 20% were considered valid cases. After data screening, 141,988 students remained in the final sample, including 48,651 students in Grade 6, 53,717 students in Grade 9, and 39,620 students in Grade 11. Overall, 60,905 cases, or 30.0% of the initial responses, were excluded during data screening.

AI literacy measure excerpt (p. 5, 2.2.1. AI Literacy):
Students’ AI literacy was assessed using a brief four-item measure developed for this study. The four items were based on the AI literacy framework proposed by Ng et al. (2021). … Based on this framework, we developed four items to represent understanding AI, using AI, evaluating AI, and AI ethics. … Each item used three ordered response categories that described increasing levels of AI-related understanding, experience, evaluation, or ethical awareness. … We therefore calculated the mean of the four items as the overall AI literacy score.

Other variables excerpt (p. 6, 2.2.2. Demographic and Contextual Variables; 2.2.3. Academic Achievement):
Demographic and family background information was collected through the same self-report questionnaire completed by the students. … Discretionary time referred to the amount of after-school time that students could arrange on their own. … Higher scores indicated more reasonable and controlled online gaming behavior. … Higher scores indicated that students used their phones more for learning or efficiency-related purposes. … The study used test scores developed by subject experts according to curriculum standards.

Data analysis excerpt (p. 7, 2.3. Data Analysis):
Independent-samples t tests and one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were used to examine differences in AI literacy by gender, grade level, place of residence, and family socioeconomic status. … Hierarchical multiple linear regression was then conducted to examine their independent associations with AI literacy. Gender, grade level, and place of residence were entered as control variables in Step 1, and the remaining study variables were entered in Step 2. … Finally, latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using the four AI literacy indicators. … The analyses treated individual students as the unit of analysis and did not explicitly adjust for clustering within schools or classes.

Group differences excerpt (p. 7, 3.2. Differences in AI Literacy Across Demographic Groups):
For gender, Grade 6 boys scored slightly higher than girls, whereas Grade 11 girls scored slightly higher than boys. No significant gender difference was found in Grade 9. … Overall, the clearest differences were found across grade level, place of residence, and family socioeconomic status, while gender differences were relatively small.

Regression excerpt (pp. 8–9, 3.4. Hierarchical Regression Analysis):
In Model 2, family socioeconomic status, academic achievement, discretionary time, sleep duration, physical activity duration, physical activity habit, online gaming habit, and mobile phone usage pattern were positively associated with AI literacy. … Online gaming time and mobile phone use time were not significantly associated with AI literacy after the other variables were included in the model. … However, the interaction coefficients and the additional explained variance were very small, suggesting that the moderating effect of grade level was limited in magnitude.

Latent profile excerpt (pp. 10–11, 3.5.2. Profile Characteristics; 3.5.3. Differences Across Profiles):
Figure 1. Mean scores across the four AI literacy indicators for the four-profile solution (n = 140,130). … Students in the high-exposure–low-reflection profile scored relatively high on understanding AI and using AI but lower on AI ethics. … Students in the low-exposure–high-reflection profile scored relatively low on understanding AI and using AI but relatively high on evaluating AI and AI ethics. … Because the profiles were based on self-reported indicators, the profile labels should be understood as descriptive summaries of the observed response patterns rather than as objective classifications of students’ actual AI competence or exposure. … The overall high-level profile included relatively more urban students and students from higher-SES families, whereas the overall low-level profile included relatively more rural students and students from lower-SES families. … Students in the high-exposure–low-reflection profile spent relatively more time on online gaming and mobile phone use and reported shorter sleep duration.

Discussion excerpt (p. 14, 4.2. Factors Associated with AI Literacy):
This result does not mean that physical activity directly increases AI literacy. A stable physical activity habit may instead reflect other characteristics, such as self-regulation, persistence, regular daily routines, and active participation in school life.

Limitations excerpt (pp. 17–18, 4.5. Limitations and Future Research):
First, the AI literacy measure included only four self-report items, with one item representing each aspect, and used a three-point response scale. These items provide broad indicators rather than complete measures of AI understanding, use, evaluation, and ethical awareness. The limited item coverage and self-reported nature of the measures may have affected measurement precision and the profiles identified by latent profile analysis. … Second, some methodological limitations should be considered. Second, some methodological limitations should be considered. Because this study used a cross-sectional design, it cannot establish causal relationships. The analyses also did not account for clustering within schools or classes because the required identifiers were unavailable. In addition, some missing values remained after data screening and were handled using available-case analysis. … Third, the participants were recruited from one city in Northeast China and included students in Grades 6, 9, and 11 rather than the entire K–12 population. The findings may therefore not generalize to other regions or grade levels.
