Design excerpt (Introduction, p. 2): To this end, this study adopts an exploratory design-based research approach through a four-stage process.

Iterative development excerpt (pp. 11–12): To ensure AIgnite’s effectiveness and adaptability in informal learning contexts, it underwent three rounds of iterative development: expert prototype evaluation, pilot testing, and expanded user testing, as illustrated in the lower part of Fig. 2. … Four design experts and one researcher conducted the evaluation through direct use and expert review, providing feedback on rule clarity, task rationale, and the balance between academic rigor and playfulness. … We invited five elementary school students (aged 10–12) to participate in a full-process trial of the AIgnite toolkit to evaluate its operability and learning effectiveness. … In the final round of evaluation, we recruited 21 students to participate in an activity conducted at the local library to comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed toolkit in a real-world application context.

Workshop setting excerpt (Evaluation and results (RQ2) > Workshop setting, pp. 13–14): A total of 42 participants (average age = 10.5 years) were recruited to evaluate the AIgnite toolkit, with approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2023009). … Recruitment was conducted via the library’s official channels, with enrollment limited to the fixed announcement period ending the day before the workshop, resulting in a total of 42 participants. … All participants were registered with parental assistance and were accompanied by their parents to and from the library on the day of the workshop. … The total duration of the workshop was approximately 2.5 h, as detailed in Fig. 7.

Measures excerpt (pp. 14–15): This component included ten single-choice questions related to core types of AI knowledge to assess students’ understanding of AI tasks, algorithms, functions, and basic principles (Zhang et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2023). … Two researchers qualitatively coded the participants’ visual outputs and verbal explanations produced during the AI Storydrawing stage. … We designed five questions rated on a 5-point Likert scale (Likert, 1932; Hinkin, 1998), and conducted paired-sample t-tests on the results. … To measure students’ subjective perceptions of their AI literacy development, we designed five items rated on a 5-point Likert scale.

Results excerpt (Results, p. 15): Two types of data were collected: (1) visual and verbal outputs produced by 42 children during the AI Storydrawing stage, with verbal responses transcribed for analysis; and (2) pre- and post-test questionnaire data from 38 children for the AI Conceptual Understanding Assessment, AI Ethical Awareness Assessment, and Self-Perceived AI Literacy Level, with four additional cases excluded due to incomplete post-test responses caused by unforeseen circumstances.

Findings excerpt (pp. 15–19): As shown in Table 1, participants demonstrated a significant improvement in their understanding of AI concepts from pre-test to post-test … Students tended to embed AI functionalities into familiar everyday objects, demonstrating a transfer of AI knowledge grounded in daily experiences. … Their designs often relied on concrete carriers, such as “robots” … As indicated in Table 2, a statistical improvement in AI ethics awareness was observed between the pre-test (M = 4.158, SD = 0.744) and post-test (M = 4.600, SD = 0.592; t = 3.798, p < .001, 95% CI [.206, .678], dz = 0.616). … Qualitative coding further revealed that students’ ethical considerations primarily focused on four dimensions: technological feasibility, fairness, potential societal risks, and recommendations for future development (for the detailed codebook, see Appendix C). … According to the self-assessment results presented in Table 3, learners’ self-perceived AI literacy levels increased from pre-test (M = 3.711, SD = 0.833) to post-test (M = 4.384, SD = 0.725; t = 4.951, p < .001, 95% CI [.398, .949], dz = 0.803), suggesting that engagement with AIgnite contributed to notable gains in students’ subjective interest, awareness, and confidence in AI literacy.

Limitations excerpt (Limitations and future work, pp. 23–24): As an exploratory design-based study, this work was intended to explore the toolkit’s potential in practice rather than seeking definitive evidence of effectiveness. … The evaluation was conducted in a single-session workshop and focused mainly on immediate post-intervention changes, so longer-term retention, transfer across contexts, and the durability of learning effects remain to be examined. … Given the short evaluation window, some observed changes may also have been influenced by factors such as test reactivity, Hawthorne effect, novelty effects, and self-report bias. … In addition, although the AI Conceptual Understanding Assessment captured students’ performance on key AI concepts, this study did not formally examine item-level properties such as item difficulty or discrimination, which should be further considered in future instrument refinement. … Because the study was conducted with voluntary participants in a library-based workshop format, self-selection may have contributed to participants’ initial interest and level of engagement, which may in turn have influenced the learning outcomes observed in the study. … This context also limits the generalizability of the findings to broader learner populations and other implementation contexts, such as regular classroom and home-based use. … Although students showed increased self-perceived AI ethics awareness and began to reflect on issues such as accessibility, privacy, responsibility, human oversight, and the appropriateness of AI use, these reflections remained preliminary. … The ethics measure also mainly captured students’ self-reported awareness, and the relatively high pre-test scores may have limited its sensitivity to more nuanced changes.

Abstract excerpt (p. 1): To address this gap, this study develops AIgnite, an AI literacy toolkit for upper primary school students in Grades 4–6. … Developed using a child-centered approach, the toolkit underwent multiple rounds of participatory co-creation and iterative prototyping with 30 participants. … A user study with another 42 participants demonstrated the toolkit’s effectiveness in conveying AI concepts and applications and in fostering ethical reflection.

Toolkit components excerpt (p. 1, Abstract): The toolkit combines AI knowledge cards with two stages supported by these cards: (i) the Board Game stage and (ii) the AI Storydrawing stage.
