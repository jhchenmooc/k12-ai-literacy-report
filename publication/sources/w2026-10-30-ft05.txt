Abstract excerpt (p. 1, Abstract):
This study assessed chemistry teachers’ research competence, examined their reported practices and perceptions concerning students’ social-emotional well-being, and investigated changes following participation in an AI-supported professional development program. … A quantitatively driven mixed-methods design with an embedded qualitative component was employed. Data were collected from 105 chemistry teachers using closed-ended questionnaire scales and two open-ended questions administered before and after a six-week professional development program. … The qualitative findings indicated a shift in teachers’ perceptions of AI from a tool used mainly for information retrieval and writing to one that could support research planning and data-informed decision-making. … The study proposes an empirically informed conceptual model integrating teacher inquiry, social-emotional monitoring, and potential AI-supported research practices.

Sample excerpt (p. 5, end of section 2 before 3 Methodology):
The study was conducted during the 2025–2026 academic year in public secondary schools in the South Kazakhstan region. A total of 105 chemistry teachers participated in the study. Participants were selected using the purposive sampling method. The main selection criteria were the participants’ currently teaching chemistry in a general secondary school, teaching experience, and informed voluntary consent to participate in the study.

Participants excerpt (p. 9, 4.1 Demographic characteristics of the participants):
Their ages ranged from 24 to 59 years, providing a heterogeneous sample across different career stages.

Research design excerpt (p. 5, 3.1 Research design):
This study employed a quantitatively driven mixed-methods design with an embedded qualitative component. Quantitative data constituted the primary strand of the study and were used to assess teachers’ research competence and the related questionnaire constructs before and after participation in the professional development program. Qualitative data were obtained from two open-ended questionnaire items administered at the same measurement points and were used to contextualize and further interpret patterns observed in the quantitative findings.

Instrument development excerpt (p. 5, 3.2 Research instruments):
Data were collected using a theory-informed questionnaire developed for the present study. … First, the initial item pool was reviewed by five experts with complementary expertise in chemistry education, educational research methodology, teacher professional development, educational psychology, and digital technologies in education. … Second, the revised questionnaire was pilot-tested with 18 chemistry teachers who met the general characteristics of the target population but were not included in the final study sample. … The questionnaire administered in the study was prepared and delivered in Kazakh, which was the working language of the participating teachers.

Questionnaire sections excerpt (pp. 6–7, 3.2 Research instruments):
Section 1 (B1) assessed Research Engagement using five modifiable items related to teachers’ participation in educational research activities. … Section 2 assessed teachers’ research competence across the principal stages of educational inquiry, including identifying pedagogical problems, formulating research questions, selecting appropriate research methods, collecting and analyzing data, interpreting findings, conducting professional reflection, and collaborating with colleagues in research activities. … Section 3 evaluated teachers’ practices of systematically monitoring students’ social-emotional well-being and incorporating this information into pedagogical decision-making. … Section 4 assessed teachers’ awareness of students’ achievement emotions across different chemistry learning contexts. … Section 5 assessed teachers’ readiness to integrate artificial intelligence into educational practice and educational research. … Finally, Section 6 consisted of two open-ended questions designed to provide a deeper understanding of teachers’ experiences and perspectives. … All quantitative items were scored on five-point Likert-type scales.

Data scope excerpt (p. 7, 3.2 Research instruments):
It should be noted that the present study did not collect student-level social-emotional data and did not apply AI algorithms to predict or classify individual students’ social-emotional states. … The questionnaire was administered anonymously, and strict confidentiality of all personal information was maintained throughout the research process.

Analysis excerpt (pp. 7–8, 3.3 Statistical data analysis):
Changes in participants’ scores following the AI-supported professional development program were examined by comparing pre-test and post-test scores using the paired-samples t-test. … Qualitative data obtained from the two open-ended questionnaire items (Questions 34 and 35) were analysed using qualitative content analysis.

Program excerpt (p. 8, 3.4 AI-supported professional development program for enhancing chemistry teachers’ research competence):
The implementation of the program was organized in stages over six weeks. Each week was dedicated to a specific stage of educational research and consisted of three interconnected parts. During the first stage, theoretical material on the research topic was reviewed, the content of the research stage was explained, and ways of using AI tools were shown. During the second stage, teachers completed authentic research activities within their own schools and chemistry classrooms. … The third stage focused on collaborative reflection and professional dialogue. … Throughout the program, AI functioned as a cognitive and methodological support tool rather than as a substitute for teachers’ professional expertise.

Program components excerpt (p. 9, 3.4 continued):
All scientific and pedagogical decisions were based on the teacher’s professional judgment and accumulated empirical evidence. It is important to note that AI-supported activities constituted only one component of the broader professional development program. The program also included research-methods training, classroom-based inquiry, collaborative reflection, professional dialogue, and the use of non-AI digital tools. The present design therefore does not permit the independent contribution of AI to the observed pre–post changes to be isolated from the effects of the other program components.

Baseline excerpt (p. 9, 4.2 Descriptive statistics of the questionnaire scales):
Mean item scores ranged from 3.50 to 3.81 on the five-point scale, with the lowest baseline score observed for teachers’ research competence.

Correlation excerpt (p. 13, 5 Discussion):
Correlation analysis further revealed statistically significant positive associations among research competence, research engagement, social-emotional monitoring, achievement emotions, and readiness to use AI. These relationships indicate that the measured constructs are associated within teachers’ broader professional practice; however, they should not be interpreted as evidence that the present study empirically established the internal dimensional structure of research competence. The constructs were specified a priori on the basis of the theoretical framework and previous research rather than derived through exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis.

Qualitative: emotional well-being excerpt (p. 10, 4.4.1):
As shown in Table 5, before the intervention teachers most frequently described students’ emotional well-being in broad terms, emphasizing its positive influence on academic achievement, learning motivation, and mastery of instructional content (37.1%). … Following the professional development program, participants’ responses became more professionally oriented. Teachers increasingly emphasized that the educational impact of emotions depends on their quality and intensity, while substantially more responses focused on the importance of systematically monitoring students’ emotional well-being and integrating this information into instructional decision-making. … Overall, the qualitative findings indicate that the post-program responses showed changes in the distribution of teachers’ reported views of the role of social-emotional information within educational research and classroom decision-making.

Qualitative: AI excerpt (p. 11, 4.4.2):
Prior to the intervention, teachers primarily viewed AI as a general-purpose instructional support tool, most commonly for preparing lessons, developing teaching materials, and searching for information. … After completing the program, participants demonstrated a considerably more sophisticated understanding of AI as a tool supporting specific stages of the educational research process. … These changes suggest that participants progressed from perceiving AI mainly as a text-generation or information-retrieval tool toward understanding its broader role in supporting the entire research cycle, including research planning, data collection, statistical analysis, interpretation of findings, and evidence-informed pedagogical decision-making.

Pre–post excerpt (pp. 11–12, 4.5 Pre-test and post-test changes following program participation):
As shown in Table 7, mean scores increased across all five constructs following completion of the program. … However, because the study employed a single-group pre-test/post-test design, these improvements should be interpreted as changes associated with program participation rather than as evidence of a causal program effect.

Pre–post discussion excerpt (p. 14, 5 Discussion):
Given the absence of a comparison group, however, these findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating that the program itself caused the observed improvements. Rather, they indicate that improvements in the measured constructs occurred during the period of program participation. Because AI was embedded within a multicomponent professional development program, the observed changes cannot be attributed specifically to AI.

Conceptual model excerpt (pp. 12–13, 4.6 Conceptual model for developing chemistry teachers’ research competence):
The model should be understood as an empirically informed, study-derived conceptual framework rather than as a formally validated structural model. … This represents a proposed application of the model rather than a procedure empirically tested in the present study.

Conclusion excerpt (p. 15, 6 Conclusion):
The empirical findings concern teachers’ self-reported research competence, social-emotional monitoring practices, perceptions of students’ achievement emotions, and readiness to use AI. … The study did not directly analyse or predict students’ social-emotional well-being using AI; therefore, AI-supported analysis of student-level social-emotional data should be regarded as a prospective component of the proposed conceptual framework that requires further empirical testing.

Limitations excerpt (pp. 14–15, 5.3 Limitations):
First, the study was conducted with a relatively small sample of 105 in-service chemistry teachers from a single region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to teachers of other subject areas, educational contexts, or national education systems. Second, the study employed a single-group pre-test/post-test design without a comparison or control group. Consequently, although statistically significant improvements were observed following participation in the professional development program, these changes cannot be attributed exclusively to the program. Alternative explanations, including repeated-measurement effects, maturation, concurrent professional experiences, or other contextual influences during the study period, cannot be ruled out. … Third, the majority of the data were derived from self-report questionnaires and participants’ reflective responses, which may be influenced by social desirability and self-perception biases. Fourth, the study assessed only short-term changes following the professional development program, and the sustainability of the observed improvements in research competence, social-emotional monitoring practices, and AI readiness was not examined. … Moreover, the multicomponent nature of the professional development program prevents the independent contribution of AI from being separated from the effects of research-methods instruction, classroom inquiry, collaborative reflection, and other digital tools.
